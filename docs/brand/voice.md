# nullrpc — positioning and voice

Use this guide for the website, repository introduction, announcements, social posts, and product copy. Visual rules and assets live in the [brand guide](README.md).

## The idea

**Affordable RPC.**

nullrpc makes the cost of accessing blockchain data central to its brand. The priorities are straightforward: fair pricing, access people can depend on, and respect for their privacy.

Lead with what people get. A developer should immediately understand the service without learning how its storage or execution pipeline works. The brand is network-neutral and has room to grow as support expands. Archive data is an important capability within that offer, rather than the boundary of the brand.

The name expresses the absence of things that get in the way: needless cost, friction, and exposure. That is the design idea behind the empty centre of the seal, the symbol. It guides how the brand behaves; it is not a literal claim of zero cost or zero data collection.

## Positioning

**Category:** RPC services.

**Approved headline and descriptor:** Affordable RPC.

**Supporting line:** Fair pricing. Privacy in focus.

**Positioning statement:** For developers and teams building on blockchain networks, nullrpc is an RPC service built around affordability and privacy. It puts useful access to network data at the center of the experience, including archive data where supported.

**Expansion direction:** Additional networks are part of the roadmap. Describe available networks and capabilities on endpoint and integration pages; the brand name and value proposition do not need to change as support grows.

The brand should feel like infrastructure: precise, sober, and secure, without being cold. It should make a practical developer service easy to understand and evaluate. Use the approved headline directly; no separate motto is needed.

## Two pillars

| Pillar | What it means for the brand | How to express it |
|---|---|---|
| **Affordable access** | The cost of using RPC should feel reasonable and easy to understand | Lead with useful access and fair pricing. When pricing is published, show actual units, inclusions, and limits plainly. |
| **Privacy focused** | Respect for users belongs in service decisions and communication | Explain data handling in plain language. Make documented privacy choices visible and easy to understand. |

These pillars are the product's priorities. Specific prices and data-handling practices should support them as those details are defined.

## Who we are speaking to

| Audience | What matters to them | Lead with |
|---|---|---|
| Developers building apps with blockchain data | Affordable access, supported networks and methods, and a straightforward integration | What they can access and how to connect |
| Teams operating products that depend on RPC | Clear pricing and support expectations | The available service, its terms, and its status |
| Developers and teams who care about privacy | Understanding how their use of a service is handled | Clear, concrete information about documented data practices |

Use familiar developer language. Define unfamiliar terms once. Treat readers as capable people making a practical choice. On integration pages, identify the network, protocol, and supported capabilities so readers can evaluate their actual use case.

## Message hierarchy

1. **What you get:** Affordable RPC.
2. **Why nullrpc:** Fair pricing and a focus on privacy.
3. **What is available:** The networks, methods, and data coverage supported by the chosen endpoint. Present archive access as a capability where available.
4. **Why you can trust the claim:** Published prices, service status, supported capabilities, and a clear explanation of data practices.
5. **What to do next:** Explore the available service or read how to connect.

Keep the first view focused on the first two points. Put technical architecture in the documentation. Keep roadmap networks distinct from available endpoints.

## Name and terminology

| Context | Rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Public name | Always lowercase, one word: **nullrpc** | “Built with nullrpc.” |
| Pronunciation | “null R P C” | Say each RPC letter individually. |
| Start of a sentence | Keep the lowercase spelling | “nullrpc puts affordable RPC first.” |
| Service category | Use **RPC** or **RPC services** | “Affordable RPC.” |
| Archive access | Present as a capability with network-specific coverage | “Explore archive access for this endpoint.” |
| Protocol and methods | Name the actual network's protocol and supported method set | “Ethereum JSON-RPC methods” on an Ethereum-compatible endpoint page |
| Code, commands and headers | Preserve their actual names, even where they differ from prose style | Package names, environment variables, RPC method names |
| Network and coverage | Name these where they help someone choose or integrate an endpoint | “Hoodi endpoint” and its available block range |
| Roadmap | Distinguish planned support from available support | “Additional networks are on the roadmap.” |

Do not use “NullRPC,” “Null RPC,” “nullRPC,” or “NULLRPC” as the prose brand name. Applying the brand does not rename commands, environment variables, or deployment resources. Do not describe every future network using Ethereum method names or assume that every network exposes JSON-RPC in the same way.

## Personality and voice

**Tone: speak like infrastructure.** Be precise, sober, and operational. State facts, numbers, and guarantees, and state a guarantee only where it is true. Write the way a status page or a runbook reads: what is available, what happened, what to do next. Avoid playful phrasing, jokes, exclamation marks, and emoji in product and brand copy. The visual identity makes the same promise; the words should not undercut it.

| Trait | How it sounds | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Precise | Exact names, units, and numbers; one claim per sentence | Approximate superlatives, “blazing fast,” unmeasured comparisons |
| Sober | State what is available, what happened, and the next useful step | Hype, panic, triumphant claims, playful or cute phrasing |
| Operational | Lead with the benefit, then the detail needed to act | Infrastructure internals before the reader understands the offer |
| Respectful | Explain price, service behavior, and data practices plainly | Vague reassurances or hiding important terms |

Use short declarative sentences and active verbs: **access, query, build, read, connect, check**. Prefer sentence case. Say “affordable” or “fair pricing” for the value proposition; avoid “cheap” where it could suggest poor quality. Use “privacy focused” to describe the priority, then use concrete policy language when explaining actual practices.

### Product copy patterns

- **Pricing:** Show the price, billing unit, included usage, and relevant limits together. Avoid using “from” without making its conditions clear.
- **Network selection:** Separate available networks from planned additions. Show the actual network name and endpoint capabilities.
- **Status:** “Last updated 35 seconds ago.” Use the actual timestamp. Pair status colors with words.
- **Unavailable:** “RPC data is unavailable. Retry the request or check service status.” Keep technical error details accessible.
- **Privacy:** Say what is collected, why, and for how long when those facts are documented. Avoid vague phrases such as “your privacy matters” as a substitute for details.
- **Empty:** “No requests in this time range.” Offer a range control if one exists.
- **Release note:** Describe the improvement and who benefits. Include the applicable network, version, and evidence for quantified claims.

## Internal claim boundaries

The values above are the agreed positioning. They do not supply an unmeasured price advantage, an uptime guarantee, or an undocumented privacy policy. Do not invent “cheapest,” savings percentages, SLAs, no-logs, no-tracking, anonymous-access, or zero-retention claims. Use specific commitments when the product owner has established them and supporting evidence is available.

Established privacy claims (October 2026; see the [Privacy Notice](https://nullrpc.dev/privacy)). Use these words, and only these, and recheck them against the running service before each new placement:

| Claim | Why it is true |
|---|---|
| **No IP addresses stored** | Keyless usage keeps a keyed hash of the client network only; no table holds an IP |
| **No request logs** | The RPC Workers run with Workers Logs off; the app and status page keep no per-request (invocation) logs |
| **No trackers** | No analytics, ad or social cookies, pixels or third-party scripts on any page |

Still never say “anonymous”, “private RPC”, “zero logs” or “we never see your IP”: Cloudflare
processes IP addresses to deliver traffic, signed transactions are forwarded to a relay, and the
app logs errors.

A nullrpc deployment serves one EVM-compatible blockchain, and Ethereum mainnet is the reference blockchain for every size, rate, and benchmark in the technical docs. Additional networks are a roadmap direction, so confirm an endpoint's network, protocol, coverage, and method support before publishing integration claims. Public value propositions do not need to explain the storage design or backfill process; technical setup documentation should describe those dependencies accurately.

## Editorial acceptance check

A reader should quickly understand **what they get, why it matters, and what to do next**. Lead with affordable RPC and the two priorities. Use the lowercase name, preserve real technical identifiers, and keep architecture details in their appropriate documentation. Match concrete price, availability, and privacy claims to real product commitments. Make network support explicit where it matters, and keep roadmap additions separate from what is available today.
